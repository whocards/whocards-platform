## Step 1: Clarity on intent

Status: agreed direction for The Third Relationship; revisit if later choices expose a conflict.

Create space for meaningful human connection and more conscious relationships at work, as AI becomes part of how we work together. Honest sharing and attentive listening invite people to explore what they trust, what they value, and how they choose to work.

Meaningful human connection is worthwhile in itself, including between strangers. More conscious relationships can mean greater clarity about expectations, healthier boundaries, or understanding someone differently. Novelty and greater closeness to AI are not goals.

Looking at processes more carefully is secondary. Useful AI knowledge can emerge from the exchange. Slowing down and focusing support the experience throughout.

We can offer an opportunity for connection in the room; carrying it into working life remains an aspiration. Discovery stays open, and participants choose what to share in the session, in feedback, or later. They don't owe the workshop an insight or commitment.

## Step 2: Understand the participant experience in this setting

Status: agreed direction on carrying attentive listening into the exploration of work and AI. The precise invitation and depth remain open.

Begin by considering what we want someone to experience in the way another person listens to them. Then consider what that experience makes possible in this particular workshop.

### What we know from Avi's experience

The opening WhoCards rounds usually help people open up, with rare exceptions. The usual ritual matters: people encounter their Question on their turn and answer without rehearsal; listeners can give their attention to the person speaking.

For this prototype, people meet at a conference and aren't an existing team. We want to offer the connection the cards make possible, with care for the depth and kind of sharing that fit this professional setting. The precise calibration remains open; personal disclosure is voluntary.

The central design question is what we invite people to do with that experience in this setting, in service of the intent. Treat the opening connection as valuable in its own right while considering how the conversation can continue into working relationships and AI.

### Direction agreed so far

After meeting another person through the cards, participants can bring the same attention to how each of them experiences AI at work. Avi agrees that listening should carry through this transition. Someone else's experience may help them see something familiar differently: how trust develops, how they involve other people, or what they expect from AI. The exercise itself and any particular discovery remain open.

The third relationship gives that exploration a focus: how the way we relate to AI becomes part of how we relate to people. Keep the conversation open to helpful experiences and uncertainty, including experiences where someone doesn't see that connection.

### The choice we are working toward

What do we want people to be able to explore together, once the opening rounds have helped them connect, that serves more conscious working relationships?

Stay with this choice until we can describe the invitation plainly. Then examine how to support it, one design decision at a time. Don't require the opening connection to produce a practical output to count as worthwhile.

## Step 3: Connect the opening experience to the next exercise

Status: Avi has specified the story order: his story, an audience share, then a share from his co-founder and fellow facilitator. The surrounding transition remains under discussion. This is design reasoning, not a revision to the prototype agenda.

### Proposed flow

1. After the paired Question rounds, invite one or two voluntary shares with the whole group. Let attention gradually return to the shared room and the facilitators.
2. Avi shares the birthday app story described below.
3. Invite one voluntary audience share, making room for a participant's own experience.
4. Avi's co-founder and fellow facilitator shares an experience of their own. The story's content remains open.
5. Use a few slides and further conversation to give the group language for the concepts they will explore.
6. Move into another exercise focused on work with AI, carrying forward the attentive listening established in the opening rounds.

### How this serves the intent

The sequence lets participants encounter each other before hearing the conceptual framing. Group shares give some of that experience a place in the wider room. Avi's story continues the personal sharing with an experience involving AI. An audience member then contributes their experience before the co-founder shares, continuing the exchange between participants and facilitators. The slides can then help people name what they are exploring together and introduce the third relationship more explicitly.

The work-and-AI exercise should continue the opportunity for meaningful human connection while inviting more conscious attention to working relationships. Reflection on processes and useful AI knowledge can arise within that purpose.

### What to protect as we develop it

Keep public shares voluntary and about the speaker's own experience; paired conversation doesn't grant permission to retell a partner's story. The two share moments serve different purposes: the first brings people back together, and the second responds to the facilitator's contribution. Neither requires a polished insight or agreement.

Facilitator input should offer a perspective people can consider, including where it doesn't fit their experience. Choose a story and concepts that help participants enter the next conversation; leave room for them to discover their own meaning. Avoid interpreting the opening exchange on their behalf.

The main design risk is that the combined stories, audience shares, and slides grow long enough to crowd out the next encounter. Consider that whole transition together when we return to the 90-minute timing.

### Opening story direction

Avi's chosen starting point is the beginning: the first time, or a memorable time, he got lost in something with AI and delivered something that felt magical.

The experience he chose: less than a week before his birthday, Avi decided he wanted a mobile app in the App Store for his birthday and made it happen. This gives the story a personal reason, a short deadline, and a concrete outcome.

Asked what those days felt like, Avi answered:

> magic pure fucking magic

Keep that feeling and his voice in the story notes. The details of how AI figured in the experience remain for Avi's telling. The account doesn't need an added complication or lesson to make it worth sharing.

The story continues the human connection by letting participants meet the facilitator through a real experience. It can make room for absorption, surprise, and the pleasure of making something. It doesn't have to demonstrate an effect on a colleague or contain a cautionary lesson. Let the actual experience determine what belongs in the story.

Avi expects many participants to recognize something of their own experience in it. Treat that as a working expectation to learn about in the prototype. Audience responses can include different experiences or no comparable AI moment; shared enthusiasm isn't a condition of belonging.

The connection we seek to deepen here is between people through sharing and listening. The story also introduces how someone experiences AI, without making greater closeness to AI an outcome.

### Next choice

Leave room for the co-founder to choose their own experience to share. Then consider how the stories and conceptual framing can carry that conversation toward working relationships. The transition into the third relationship remains open; don't require either facilitator's story to explain the whole concept.

## Step 4: Open up the conversation through a group activity

Status: parked while we examine timing. Avi found the creation options below too elaborate. A simpler activity might involve submitting something through a form or generating something with AI; the mechanism remains open. Participants should first share with each other, then create or contribute something together. A slightly larger group than the opening pairs feels promising; the activity and group size aren't agreed yet. Keep the earlier options as discussion history, not the current shortlist.

### Starting proposal: real experiences in groups of four

The proposed focus is what changes between people when AI becomes part of their work. Begin with particular experiences so participants can hear how the same technology enters different working relationships.

Two opening pairs could join to form a group of four. This retains a familiar listener while introducing other perspectives. Four is a design proposal to try, not an established optimum.

Give each person an initial opportunity to share a concrete moment when AI's presence, use, or deliberate non-use mattered in work involving someone else. People can draw on the receiving side of AI use; they don't need to be AI users themselves. Passing remains available. Prepare any actual Questions later through the `whocards-questions` skill.

After that first round, invite the group to follow a difference or connection among their experiences that interests them. The shift into conversation matters: participants can respond to one another, clarify what they heard, and explore why something experienced as helpful by one person might feel different to another. They speak from their own experience and leave absent colleagues' motives open.

The activity's purpose is connection and more conscious understanding. Agreement, advice, a repaired process, and an action plan aren't required outcomes. A closing opportunity to name something they now see differently can remain personal and optional; any public sharing should respect whose story it is.

### What this starting proposal offers

Real accounts continue the personal sharing already underway and let participants bring their own stakes into the room. The larger group introduces more perspectives, while the initial turn for each person protects space before open conversation begins. The main risk is drifting into general opinions about AI or unsolicited problem-solving; the brief should keep attention on lived experiences and relationships.

A shared fictional workplace situation is an alternative if we want everyone to explore the same event or if people struggle to find an experience they can share. That offers a common starting point with less personal exposure, though it may invite abstract debate. We haven't chosen it as the main activity.

The conversation-only proposal above is background for the next iteration. Avi's clarification adds a shared act of creation. Develop options where what people make depends on hearing each other, keeping the current 90-minute constraint in view.

### Creation options to discuss with Tamas

These are original activity proposals, not tested formats or agreed workshop instructions. Groups of four remain a possible size. Each option starts with an opportunity for everyone to contribute an experience or perspective, then asks the group to make something that draws on those contributions. Participants choose what to disclose and what may travel beyond their group. Develop the actual opening Questions later with the `whocards-questions` skill.

#### Option A: A scene from working life

First, participants share moments when AI mattered in work involving other people, including what the experience meant to them. The group then creates a short fictional scene or storyboard drawing on something each person contributed. They invent the characters and situation rather than retelling a participant's identifiable story.

The scene shows how one AI-related event can feel different to the people involved. It can hold excitement and uncertainty without requiring a conflict or a tidy ending. The group chooses what to leave unresolved. A storyboard is enough; acting is optional.

The shared creation gives people a reason to understand one another's perspective well enough to represent it. Watch for one person's dramatic story taking over or the group inventing motives for absent colleagues. This option has the most room for imagination and play.

#### Option B: A guide to working with us when AI is involved

First, each participant shares an experience that helps explain a preference or need in working with others around AI. The group creates a one-page guide representing these particular people: what helps them participate, what they want others to understand, and where their preferences differ.

The result can include individual notes alongside shared ground. It isn't a policy for their employers; these participants aren't an existing team. Each person checks that the guide represents what they actually meant.

Creating the guide requires people to listen closely enough to describe each other with care. It makes expectations discussable, though the brief needs to keep it grounded in experiences so it doesn't become a list of generic principles. This option connects most directly to more conscious working relationships.

#### Option C: A map of AI's effects between people

First, participants share concrete moments when AI use affected how work moved between people. Together they draw a map of a fictional piece of work, using their accounts to include different perspectives along the way. Show people, AI use, and what each person gives, receives, or needs to understand.

Each participant contributes a detail from their experience that changes or adds to the map. Mark differing interpretations and unknowns rather than making the group settle them. People can describe receiving AI-assisted work without using AI themselves.

The creation makes the third relationship visible: a choice involving AI can reach beyond its immediate user. This is the most explicit way to explore that concept, though it risks drifting into process analysis. Keep people's experiences central to what the map shows.

#### Option D: A small ritual for working together with AI

First, participants share occasions when they felt included, supported, or disconnected as AI became part of work. The group invents a small interaction that responds to something they heard across those accounts, then demonstrates it with an imagined workplace moment.

They create a rough prototype of a human practice, such as a conversation around handing work to another person. Each participant explains whether the proposed ritual would serve what they shared; differences can remain visible or lead to alternative versions.

This offers a concrete act of making and trying something together. It also moves furthest toward the secondary aim of examining processes, so consider whether that is the right emphasis for this prototype. Trying it in the workshop creates no commitment to introduce it at work.

### Earlier assessment of the options (parked)

The scene and the guide are the strongest starting candidates for discussion: the scene offers imaginative perspective-taking, while the guide makes understanding the actual people in the group the shared task. The map foregrounds the third relationship concept; the ritual foregrounds a possible practice.

Across the options, the creation should give listening a purpose without turning connection into a performance requirement. A rough or unfinished result can still hold a worthwhile encounter. Decide separately whether to use AI during the activity; each proposal can start with paper, leaving attention available for one another. Tool setup and AI output quality shouldn't determine participation.

Revisit the activity after examining how much time it has. Prefer a simpler design in response to Avi's feedback above.

## Step 5: Check the 90-minute timing

Status: Avi finds the overall allocation mostly reasonable. The round timing below is confirmed; the number of rounds and final allocation remain open. This schedule doesn't change the local prototype or its Google Doc.

The existing prototype allocates 10 minutes to arrival, 12 to general Questions, 15 to AI Questions, eight to conceptual framing, five to the break, 25 to the core activity, and 15 to closing. That totals 90 minutes, with 45 minutes before the break. The newly discussed sequence needs explicit room for Avi's story, one audience share, and Tamas's story.

### Proposed allocation

| Elapsed time | Minutes | What happens                                                                                              |
| ------------ | ------: | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 00–08        |       8 | Welcome, arrival, WhoCards and facilitator introductions, participation guidance.                         |
| 08–28        |      20 | One general Question round and one AI-focused round in pairs, with brief voluntary group shares included. |
| 28–38        |      10 | Avi's birthday app story, one audience share, then Tamas's story.                                         |
| 38–45        |       7 | A few slides and facilitator input to introduce the third relationship and frame the activity.            |
| 45–50        |       5 | Break.                                                                                                    |
| 50–75        |      25 | Small-group activity; form submission or AI generation remains undecided.                                 |
| 75–90        |      15 | Reflection and closing shares, thanks, and the short feedback form. Protect three minutes for the form.   |
| **Total**    |  **90** |                                                                                                           |

Each block includes its instructions and transitions. Avi clarified the fixed Question-round rhythm:

- Each person has **two minutes** to answer, so a paired round has four minutes of answers.
- Allow **one minute between rounds** to swap partners and give people who need a few extra seconds room to finish. Don't schedule another task into that minute.
- Participants first encounter their Question on their turn. Partner changes are part of the round sequence.

Two rounds therefore use nine minutes for answers and the transition between them; three use 14 minutes; four use 19 minutes. These figures exclude initial instructions and whole-room shares. In the provisional 20-minute block, two rounds leave 11 minutes for instructions, group shares, and returning attention to the facilitators. Three would leave six minutes; four would leave only one. The table retains the proposed one general round and one AI round for now; we haven't chosen to add rounds or reassign the remaining time.

The ten-minute story block could allow about three minutes for each facilitator, two for the audience share, and two for introductions and transitions. This is a pacing proposal, not a script.

### What this allocation makes visible

This keeps the break, the 25-minute activity, and the 15-minute closing. It finds ten minutes for the stories by reducing arrival from ten to eight, the combined Question block from 27 to 20, and conceptual framing from eight to seven. Avi finds the overall balance mostly reasonable. The fixed two-minute turns clarify how much of the Question block is actual paired conversation; its remaining time still needs allocation. Don't silently add stories to the old schedule.

The activity begins at minute 50. The opening conversations already serve the primary purpose of connection, so they count as part of the workshop's core value. Still, the story and slides section needs a clear end if the later group work is to retain its space.

Twenty-five minutes is the full activity allowance, including forming groups, explaining the task, sharing, making or generating something, and submitting it. The eventual design needs to fit that whole window. AI setup or a separate presentation from every group would consume part of it; neither has an additional allocation here.

Use this broadly supported balance when returning to the design. If the activity needs more time, identify the tradeoff explicitly while preserving the agreed five-minute break and short closing feedback form.
