# Comparable workshops and psychological tensions around AI at work

Research date: 9 September 2026. Exploratory research for the 90-minute mixed-group prototype. The requested researcher agent hit a usage limit; the primary agent completed this research directly.

## Current direction and earlier recommendation

The current prototype develops the original third-relationship concept: **how one's relationship with AI affects human relationships**. Pressure and pace remain possible themes. The earlier research recommendation was to focus on how pressure to keep up affects attention and working relationships. That remains an alternative, not the current center of the workshop.

Both approaches can accommodate enthusiasm and relief as well as strain. Participants need not agree that speed is harmful, that AI use should increase, or that their employer is failing. These are design recommendations, not validated psychological constructs or proven workshop interventions.

In this small comparison, several providers connect emotions to adoption, resilience, or practical changes. A plausible position for The Third Relationship is a place to examine the experience of working with AI without requiring a particular attitude toward it. This is an inference from these examples, not a claim that nobody else offers such a space.

## Tensions worth being able to name

The plain-language names below are suggested descriptions, not diagnoses or assessment scales. Give participants room to reject or rename them. Hosts can keep the full list in their notes and introduce only the few themes the room actually raises.

### Pressure to keep up

**Research:** Ye and Ranganathan's in-progress research followed one roughly 200-person technology company for eight months, with more than 40 interviews. They observed expanding task scope, reduced stopping points, and more simultaneous work. The researcher describes how initially exciting capability gains could become new expectations. This is a case study, not a causal estimate for all workplaces. [Interview with the researcher, UC Berkeley](https://vcresearch.berkeley.edu/news/does-ai-actually-free-workers-time).

**In the room:** allow a person to trace one moment when they felt rushed, eager to continue, or unable to stop. Help distinguish an explicit request from an assumed expectation and a self-imposed standard. The listener reflects the experience before suggesting a remedy.

### Uncertainty about one's value

**Research:** Pew surveyed 5,273 employed U.S. adults in October 2024. Its February 2025 report found 32% expected workplace AI to reduce their future job opportunities. This measures expectations, not actual displacement or clinical anxiety. [Pew's original report and methodology](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2025/02/25/u-s-workers-are-more-worried-than-hopeful-about-future-ai-use-in-the-workplace/).

**In the room:** invite reflection on contributions a person values and wants others to recognize. Changes in pride or professional identity are possible themes to explore, not findings established by that survey. Don't require people to disclose employment insecurity to strangers or reassure them that their role is safe.

### Worry about being judged for using AI

**Research:** Reif, Larrick, and Soll report four preregistered experiments with 4,439 participants. AI users anticipated and received negative evaluations concerning ability and effort; the studies also examined consequences for candidate assessment. The work establishes a possible social penalty under its experimental conditions, not a current prevalence estimate for real workplaces. [Original PNAS paper](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2426766122).

**In the room:** explore expectations about credit, effort, and disclosure through a volunteered or fictional situation. Don't turn the activity into a confession of hidden AI use. A person may face conflicting expectations about using AI and demonstrating their own competence.

### Doubt about one's own judgment

**Research:** Lee and colleagues surveyed 319 knowledge workers about 936 examples of AI use. Greater confidence in AI was associated with less self-reported critical thinking; respondents also described thinking shifting toward checking and integrating outputs. The study does not demonstrate permanent loss of ability. [Original CHI 2025 publication summary](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/).

**In the room:** let participants reflect on where they want to retain judgment and what makes it easier to pause before accepting an answer. Hold technical verification training for another setting. The relational theme is agency and confidence in one's contribution.

### Missing the contact that work used to involve

**Research:** Tang and colleagues' four studies, totaling 794 participants, examined AI interaction, social affiliation, and loneliness using mixed methods. Their model includes both increased helping of coworkers and loneliness. It does not establish that every AI interaction displaces a colleague, and the 2023 systems studied should not be treated as identical to current conversational assistants. [Original paper abstract, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37307359/).

**In the room:** let people notice which human interactions have become less frequent, which they miss, and which they are relieved to avoid. Discuss opportunities for contact without collecting personal mental-health histories or assuming more interaction is always better.

### Expectations changing before people have discussed them

**Research:** the established technostress literature studies strain associated with workplace technology and organizational responses. Ragu-Nathan and colleagues tested their model with 608 ICT users in 2008; it predates generative AI. [Original paper abstract](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/isre.1070.0165).

**Interpretation for this workshop:** a changing handoff or review process may carry an unspoken expectation about response time or responsibility. “Processes are breaking faster than we can repair them” remains the user's hypothesis to explore, not a conclusion established by this study. Have participants describe an observed change, its effect on a relationship, and what they need clarified. A mixed group cannot redesign an absent team's process.

## Evidence that keeps the premise honest

AI can also create breathing room. Dillon and colleagues' six-month field experiment across 66 firms and 7,137 workers randomly allocated access to an integrated AI tool. Their November 2025 abstract reports less email time and less work outside regular hours among treated workers who used the tool, while finding no wider shift in task quantity or composition. The usage-based finding should not be described as an effect on every assigned worker. [Authors' working paper, version 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11436v4).

These findings and the Berkeley observations concern different settings and designs. Neither establishes a general tradeoff between short-term growth and relationship health. The workshop should allow participants to distinguish what AI made easier from what workplace expectations did with that extra capacity.

## Bringing themes into a 90-minute mixed group

Use the first AI conversation to collect experience before introducing labels. Ask for voluntary themes rather than other people's stories. Then offer a few plain-language descriptions that fit what the room raised, including room for a positive experience or a different description entirely. This adapts the participation principles in our [facilitation research](facilitation-research.md).

For the core, use a listening exercise around one moment per person. The speaker describes what happened and what mattered; the listener checks their understanding and separates an observation from an assumption. The speaker chooses whether to explore a need, a boundary, or a conversation to take back to work. Avoid unsolicited advice and interpretations of absent colleagues' motives.

For this pilot, defer process redesign, tool recommendations, and a broad lesson on the psychology of AI. Keep the five-minute break and the short feedback form. A useful evaluation would examine whether participants could express their experience, felt heard, and could name something they want to discuss afterward. A form cannot establish lasting improvement in relationships.

No source reviewed here tests this exact 90-minute format. Confidence is moderate that the proposed focus fits the stated intent; the prototype must test whether participants find it relevant. The [page and format review](third-relationship-page-review.md) records the current third-relationship direction and the earlier pressure-and-pace alternative.
