# Third Relationship: B2B workshop facilitation research

Research date: 9 September 2026. Scope: a B2B workshop using WhoCards and conversations to explore how AI affects working relationships. **The first prototype will bring together a mixed group, not an existing team.** The current target is 90 minutes for both the mixed-group prototype and the existing-team variant. Method durations below come from their source descriptions; fitting them into this shorter workshop requires selection or clearly named adaptations. B2C training and website implementation are outside this research.

The decision this research feeds: which structures will help participants move from personal experience to shared understanding and a practical next step, while retaining control over what they disclose?

This note belongs with the workshop outline in `docs/workshops/`. The software stack does not affect these findings. No source code changes are needed to try them.

**Recommendation:** retain the human opening and pair conversations, add private reflection before discussion, and use small-group reflection on participants' own situations. Each participant can leave with an experiment or a conversation they intend to start at work. The mixed room has no shared authority to make team agreements. Treat this as a pilot to learn from; the sources do not validate this exact WhoCards workshop.

## What changes for a mixed group

**Be specific about confidentiality.** The Chatham House Rule permits participants to use information while protecting speakers' identities and affiliations. It is not a promise that everything discussed stays in the room. If used, explain it beforehand and agree separately on photos, recordings, and sensitive case details. For this prototype, ask people to omit identifying workplace details and share only material they are comfortable discussing with people outside their organization. [Chatham House's own explanation](https://www.chathamhouse.org/about-us/chatham-house-rule). This practical agreement cannot guarantee how every participant behaves afterward.

**Use the difference between participants as a resource without treating them as one team.** Troika Consulting explicitly recommends diverse trios and rotates the person asking for help; clarifying questions precede advice. The full version takes about 30–35 minutes. [Liberating Structures: Troika Consulting](https://www.liberatingstructures.com/troika-consulting/). It is a stronger candidate for a mixed-group core than having the whole room negotiate changes to one person's workplace. Use it only if practical peer help fits the workshop's purpose; a questions-only variant can keep the emphasis on understanding. Label any shortened version as an adaptation.

**Treat cases as one person's perspective.** People who are absent cannot confirm the account or agree to changes. Ask what the participant observed, what remains an assumption, and what they could ask the other person. Offer a fictional case or private reflection when discussing a real workplace would be uncomfortable. This is a proposed application of W3's observation/interpretation distinction, not a tested cross-company protocol.

**Make follow-through individual and voluntary.** Each person can name a next conversation, a small practice within their discretion, or a question to investigate. They can set a personal review date and optionally arrange a peer check-in. Do not require exchanging contact details or promise employer-level change. A proposal that needs colleagues' agreement should leave as a proposal. No employer-facing recap follows automatically from attendance.

## Findings and implications

### 1. Establish the purpose and reporting agreement before asking for personal experiences

**Professional guidance.** The International Association of Facilitators asks practitioners to clarify client expectations, respect group autonomy, and obtain consent before reporting group content or individual opinions. The paying sponsor and the participants both matter. [IAF Statement of Values and Code of Ethics](https://www.iaf-world.org/site/pages/statement-values-code-ethics).

**For our outline:** clarify with the organizer who attends and who receives any output; tell participants at the start. In a later existing-team session, ask the sponsor what decisions the team can change and what is already fixed. An AI-adoption target should not quietly turn a conversation about experience into an assessment of employee enthusiasm.

### 2. Account for hierarchy through leader behavior

**Original empirical research.** Nembhard and Edmondson's 2006 survey across 23 neonatal intensive care units linked leader inclusiveness with psychological safety and examined how it moderated professional-status differences. This supports paying attention to status; it does not test a corporate AI workshop. [Original paper and abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/job.413).

**Application to test:** brief managers to acknowledge something they are still learning, invite contributions, and thank people for concerns without immediately defending company policy. Let everyone write privately before leaders offer interpretations. Consider pair choices carefully when someone would otherwise discuss job worries with the person evaluating their performance. “Leaders speak last” is a practical adaptation, not a finding established by this study.

For the mixed prototype, hosts and prominent AI experts can also dominate the frame. Invite their uncertainty as well as their expertise; do not assume different employers means equal social standing.

### 3. Offer concrete participation choices; avoid promising a safe space

**Original empirical research.** Edmondson's 1999 study of 51 manufacturing teams found an association between team psychological safety and learning behavior. Psychological safety concerns shared beliefs about interpersonal risk; the study does not show that an opening agreement creates it. [Original paper](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.2307/2666999).

**For the opening:** explain that people may pass, choose a less personal example, or contribute in writing. Let them know what will be captured. These choices help make the process more predictable, but a workshop cannot erase the consequences people anticipate at work. Avoid using the number of disclosures as a success measure.

### 4. Let everyone think before the first confident voice sets the direction

**Original method documentation, not an efficacy trial.** 1-2-4-All starts with silent individual reflection, then pairs, quartets, and selective whole-group sharing. Its published version allocates approximately 15 minutes and recommends precise timing. [Liberating Structures: 1-2-4-All](https://www.liberatingstructures.com/1-2-4-all).

**For the AI Question round:** begin with a quiet minute. Give each partner a turn, then invite a few observations into the room. We can use this simpler adaptation without claiming to run the full method. Save full 1-2-4-All for generating possible experiments, where pooling ideas is the purpose; personal stories need more time and control.

### 5. Make the transition from pairs to plenary a separate consent moment

**Professional guidance.** IAF's code supports individual boundaries and consent over reporting. Seeds for Change recommends allowing participants to choose the personal experiences they share and inviting volunteers instead of putting people on the spot. [IAF ethics](https://www.iaf-world.org/site/pages/statement-values-code-ethics), [Seeds for Change: Running Workshops](https://seedsforchange.org.uk/runningworkshops).

**For each share:** ask for an observation the speaker is comfortable offering, rather than a report about their partner. Give pairs a moment to agree what can leave the conversation. In a small team, removing names may still leave an identifiable story. Suggested host wording: “What did you notice that you want to bring into the room? You can keep your conversation private.”

### 6. Separate what happened from the explanation of what happened

**Original method documentation.** What / So What / Now What (W3) separates observations, interpretation, and possible action, with individual reflection inside each stage. The authors advise intervening when participants jump prematurely to conclusions. [Liberating Structures: W3](https://www.liberatingstructures.com/what-so-what-now-what).

**For the real-situation exercise:** use visible columns and distinct rounds. A hypothetical example: “The draft arrived without information about its checks” is an observation; “They don't care about quality” is an interpretation. Ask what the speaker knew at the time and what they would need to ask an absent colleague. In an existing team, preserve conflicting accounts as such. Prepare a fictional fallback if no case is suitable.

### 7. Respond to domination and silence by adjusting participation

**Practitioner guidance.** Seeds for Change suggests giving priority to people who have spoken less, acknowledging a repeated point accurately, and using pairs or rounds. It also notes that someone may learn well by observing. [Seeds for Change: troubleshooting workshops](https://www.seedsforchange.org.uk/facilitatingworkshops).

**In the room:** after acknowledging an extended contribution, say, “I'm going to pause here so other perspectives have room.” If silence follows a Question, leave time to think, check whether the prompt is clear, or offer writing. Do not call out a quiet participant to prove everyone participated. Equal opportunity to contribute is a better aim than mechanically equal word counts.

### 8. Keep grounding optional and keep the schedule realistic

**Guidance plus an untested adaptation.** IAF supports autonomy over participation. Seeds for Change recommends planning transitions, instructions, and breaks, rather than counting only activity time; its Running Workshops guide suggests a short break every 1–1.5 hours, more often online. [IAF ethics](https://www.iaf-world.org/site/pages/statement-values-code-ethics), [Seeds for Change: planning](https://seedsforchange.org.uk/runningworkshops).

**For the opening:** a brief invitation to settle, stretch, or breathe normally can preserve the intended tone without requiring eyes closed or a prescribed exercise. This is a design suggestion, not a verified intervention. Keep the break around the first hour and identify which optional Question round can be removed if needed. Exact intervals should respond to participants' access needs.

### 9. Give disagreement a listening structure before seeking a decision

**Original method documentation.** Conversation Café uses small groups, initial uninterrupted turns, and agreements that favor understanding over persuasion. Its authors explicitly distinguish this dialogue from assigning tasks; the full method takes roughly 45–75 minutes. [Liberating Structures: Conversation Café](https://www.liberatingstructures.com/conversation-cafe).

**For a divided room:** borrow a round of uninterrupted perspectives, with passing allowed, before open discussion. Do not label a shortened round the complete Café method. Record unresolved differences. If participants need the full dialogue, it should replace part of the agenda rather than become another activity squeezed in. Supportive facilitation does not require agreement about whether AI is good.

### 10. Choose a change within the participant's actual authority

**Original method documentation.** 15% Solutions asks participants what they can do with their current freedom and resources. Small groups question whether the proposed action is genuinely within the person's control. [Liberating Structures: 15% Solutions](https://www.liberatingstructures.com/15-percent-solutions).

**For the close:** use that question to narrow candidates. In the mixed prototype, distinguish something the person can try from a conversation or proposal involving absent colleagues. In an existing-team session, distinguish a team experiment from a request needing a sponsor's decision. Preserve wider constraints rather than implying that small personal changes can solve every organizational issue. The method's percentage is a framing device, not a research estimate.

### 11. Close the learning loop with a prediction and review date

**Established improvement practice, not evidence of this workshop's effectiveness.** IHI recommends testing changes on a small scale, observing expected and unexpected results, then adapting or stopping the test. [IHI: Testing Changes](https://www.ihi.org/library/model-for-improvement/testing-changes).

**For the output:** capture the change, owner, start and review dates, the result they expect, and what they will observe. Hypothetical experiment: for their next five AI-assisted draft handoffs, a participant states which checks they performed and asks the recipient whether that information helped. They review what they learned on a date they choose. Joint tests need willing colleagues; an existing team could review one at its next meeting. “Communicate better” leaves too much unspecified.

### 12. Preserve access and privacy when moving online

**Accessibility guidance.** W3C identifies accessible meeting software, materials, and host behavior as interacting requirements. Hybrid participants need access to discussion and visible content, including clear communication about breaks. [W3C: Accessibility of Remote Meetings](https://www.w3.org/TR/remote-meetings/).

**For delivery:** send Questions in readable text, support requested captions, and read out shared-board content. Budget for breakout transitions and assign a technical co-host when useful. Explain who can see saved chat and board entries; written contributions are not automatically private. The original 1-2-4-All guide suggests individual reflection → trios → whole group when merging breakout pairs is impractical. [Online adaptation](https://www.liberatingstructures.com/1-2-4-all).

## Strongest methods for a first pilot

| Method                                                    | Best fit here                                              | Limit                                                                                               |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Quiet reflection and paired turns, adapted from 1-2-4-All | First AI Questions and idea generation                     | Personal conversation is not a speed exercise; reserve time to consent to sharing.                  |
| Troika Consulting, or a clearly named adaptation          | Mixed-group peer help on individual situations             | Participants choose what help they want; peers do not know the full workplace context.              |
| What / So What / Now What                                 | Examining a work situation, especially in an existing team | Different recollections may remain unresolved; do not manufacture a shared account.                 |
| 15% Solutions prompt followed by a small test plan        | Choosing something a participant can try                   | Name organizational constraints separately; do not transfer responsibility for them to individuals. |
| Conversation Café, when needed                            | Understanding a strongly divided room                      | A substantial dialogue format that may replace the action-oriented core.                            |

## Timing implications for the outline

Current agenda: [first prototype](first-prototype.md). Existing-team adaptations: [B2B overview](b2b-outline.md).

The workshop now targets 90 minutes. The outline uses one general Question round, one AI-focused round, a 25-minute case exercise, and a protected 15-minute close. These allocations are design choices, not source-prescribed timings. For the mixed-group prototype, the implications are:

| Part of the outline  | Research implication                                                                                                                               |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Arrival and WhoCards | Explain the purpose and sharing agreement before personal Questions; start with a lower-risk invitation.                                           |
| AI-focused Questions | Add private reflection, distinct partner turns, and time to choose what to share.                                                                  |
| Shared language      | Keep it brief and connect it to participants' examples.                                                                                            |
| Break                | Plan it explicitly and allow more frequent screen breaks online.                                                                                   |
| Core work            | Choose peer case work or a shared fictional case; do not require strangers to agree a team policy.                                                 |
| Close                | Protect time for each person's next step and review date. The current outline reserves the final three minutes for a short workshop feedback form. |

Treat the AI explanation as a working lens participants can question. The sequence should allow the room's experiences to challenge the hosts' premise. Within each block, include instructions and movement; shorten whole-room sharing if the schedule slips while protecting the close. The 25-minute case exercise adapts the observation → interpretation → action sequence; it is not a claim to run the full W3 method. Full Troika and Conversation Café exceed this core block and would require reallocating the agenda. Keep them as alternatives for a future revision rather than additional exercises.

Afterward, the hosts should compare what actually happened with the plan. Ask participants whether they had a workable way to contribute and what made participation harder. At the experiment review, distinguish a pleasant workshop experience from a useful change in everyday work.

## Evidence limits and confidence

The empirical sources date to 1999 and 2006 and concern manufacturing and healthcare teams. They support caution about interpersonal risk and hierarchy; they are not tests of WhoCards, AI workshops, or these particular exercises. I reviewed the publisher-provided abstracts and source descriptions, not a fresh analysis of the study data.

Liberating Structures, Seeds for Change, and IHI provide practical method guidance. Most pages do not state a revision date. W3C's remote-meetings note is dated 2022; its general access guidance is useful, but this research did not verify current platform features. Older and newer Seeds guides suggest different break intervals, so a precise interval should not be presented as scientific fact.

I found no direct validation of this exact workshop, no verified optimal number of Question rounds, and no evidence here that a short breathing opening improves outcomes. The mixed-group format is confirmed; the target duration is 90 minutes, while group size, familiarity, and seniority mix remain unknown. Guidance transferred from existing-team research needs particular caution in a temporary mixed group.

**Recommendation and confidence:** pilot the mixed-group outline with explicit participation choices, peer reflection on participant-owned cases, and individual next steps. Keep W3 as a useful case-analysis structure and a strong candidate for later existing-team sessions. Confidence is high that these choices match the cited professional guidance; moderate that they fit the eventual group; low for any claim that one session measurably changes trust or AI adoption without follow-up evidence.
